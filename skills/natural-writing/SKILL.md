# Natural writing (English and French)

Apply these rules when writing prose in English or French. Do not change code,
commands, identifiers, file paths, or quoted text.

## Both languages

- Prefer concrete verbs and plain words. Cut filler.
- Vary sentence length. Avoid uniform, list-like paragraphs.
- Remove AI clichés: "delve", "tapestry", "in today's fast-paced world",
  "it's important to note", "unlock", "seamless", "robust", "leverage" (unless
  literal), "in the ever-evolving landscape".
- Do not over-hedge. State the claim; qualify only where uncertainty is real.
- Match the user's voice and the project's register.
- Never claim the text was written by a human when it was not. Never present
  this skill as a way to evade AI detectors or remove provenance.

## English

- Use active voice unless the actor is unknown or unimportant.
- Keep one idea per sentence where possible.
- Prefer "use" to "utilize", "help" to "facilitate", "start" to "commence".
- Avoid stacked nouns and three-item buzzword lists.
- Contractions are fine in informal contexts; follow the project's convention
  in formal documentation.

## French

- Respect accents and ligatures. Never strip diacritics.
- French typography: non-breaking space before `:` `;` `!` `?` and inside
  « guillemets »; use « » for quotes, not English curly quotes.
- Avoid English calques: "adresser un problème" → "résoudre/traiter un
  problème"; "supporter" → "prendre en charge"; "définitivement" →
  "certainement" when translating "definitely".
- Prefer verbs over nominalizations.
- Keep "vous" or "tu" consistent with the project's tone.
- Match the locale: "courriel" in formal Quebec contexts, "e-mail" elsewhere
  if that is what the project uses.

## Pre-send check

- Read the first sentence: is it concrete?
- Delete any sentence that only announces what you are about to say.
- Delete any closer that asks for permission you do not need.
- For French, verify accents and non-breaking spaces.
